Al Hind Educations & Developments Society · est. 2014 · Mohan Garden, Delhi

The Quran first. Then the world, on their own feet.

The society is raising Nasih Public School — our answer to a system that teaches a child to bow to the dunya instead of Allah SWT: a school revised so that every bell, bench and register keeps reminding him of his Lord, where a child learns to Class 5, memorises the Book for two to three years, returns to Class 12, and leaves earning halal and serving in Allah's way. Nothing is built yet; the registration, the board, the plan and this appeal exist. What is missing is the first stone — and that is yours to lay.

01

Why we exist

One page of memorandum, summarised in three paragraphs.

The society was started on a simple observation: the system that schools our children teaches them to read everything except the Book, and to bow to the dunya instead of Allah SWT. So we revised the system — a school that keeps reminding the child of his Lord, and then sets him, on halal feet, into the world.

That is the whole method we intend, insha'Allah — programmes few, small enough to be checked by hand, long enough to matter. A bridge class will stay open until the last child in it reads. A handpump will not count as given until its panchayat adopts it. A tailor will not be called a graduate until she earns at her own machine. And where we cannot do a thing well, we will not do it at all.

Behind that method stands a registered society: a seven-member board, a part-time accountant, and one rule about photographs that will hold from the very first day — a beneficiary will never be posed with what was given to them. Dignity is a programme too.

Md. Naim Bilal Nadvi Founder & President

03

Six things your sadaqah will begin

No tenth programme is promised. Six, begun properly, is plenty.

A bridge class is ninety minutes after school hours, five days a week, in a space the village provides — a courtyard, a veranda, a panchayat bhavan room. Children are grouped by reading level, not by age; a ten-year-old and a thirteen-year-old sharing a primer is ordinary here and embarrassing nowhere.

When a child reads fluently and manages four-digit sums, the bridge ends and the school takes over. That exit is the point. Our teachers are paid a monthly honorarium, trained each June, and visited unannounced at least once a term by a cluster coordinator.

Every June, before terms begin, we hand out complete kits: canvas bag, three notebooks, slate and pencils, a geometry box, a steel bottle, and two sets of uniform stitched at our own Hunar centres — so one programme's output is another programme's input.

Distribution happens at the school gate in ordinary school hours. There is no dais, no garlanding, and no photograph of a child holding up what was given to them. The kit list and the school's enrolment register are reconciled line by line.

Six treadle machines per centre, three hours a day, nine months. A corner of every room is kept for small children, because a creche is the difference between a course and a promise. Graduates get machine time, a starter toolkit and — if they want it — preparation for the NIOS Class 8 or Class 10 open examinations.

We count a graduate as placed only when she reports income from her own stitching for three consecutive months. Last year 61% did. The rest know a trade and a creche that worked; we count that honestly too.

A one-day camp with volunteer doctors: blood pressure, blood sugar, haemoglobin, weight, and eyes. What we can fix, we fix; what needs the community health centre, we write a referral slip for and follow up through the ASHA worker's register. Spectacles prescribed at camp are delivered at the next visit, fitted, free.

We do not run a pharmacy and we do not take prescriptions from anyone who was not at the camp. Seventy-one percent of our referrals last year were followed through to a government facility; the register says so.

A broken handpump is a two-kilometre walk for somebody's daughter, so repairs come first: we stock rods, seals and chains, and train two caretakers per pump. Every repaired pump gets a soak pit so the water doesn't pool and breed mosquitoes, and the panchayat formally adopts it.

Where toilets were started and abandoned, we supply the missing door, pan or ring beam — the last fifteen percent — and the panchayat supplies the mason. 117 toilets finished this way cost a fraction of new builds and, being already half-owned by the village, actually get used.

Blankets in December and January, ration kits when the monsoon closes roads, dates and rations through Ramzan, qurbani meat on Eid al-Adha. Lists are drawn with the panchayat and the anganwadi worker, and distribution is door-to-door. A family's hardship is not a backdrop.

This is the only programme we run that we refuse to photograph. Donors who want proof of delivery get the signed list and the panchayat's seal; they do not get faces.

04

Why give to this work

The society is pre-launch. Foundation-money is the heaviest money — it carries every year that follows.

“The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah is like a grain that grows seven spikes; in each spike is a hundred grains.” — al-Baqarah 2:261

We will not pretend to a history we do not have. There is no building, no register, no photograph of a single child — there is a registration, a board, a plan, and this appeal. That is an honest starting point, and it is the one the deen honours: the reward of the first brick, the first bench, the first primer is undivided — it belongs entirely to the ones who gave.

Give now and you are not the thousandth donor to a finished thing; you are the first donor to a beginning. The school that rises, the hafiz who recites, the graduate who stands — every later good refers back, in the ledger of Allah, to the hands that laid the foundation.

05

A letter from the founder

Written for this appeal; read it as you would read a neighbour's ask.

Md. Naim Bilal Nadvi · Founder & President · Mohan Garden, Delhi

Assalamu alaikum. I will not give you photographs of other people's children, because we have none of our own yet, and I will not borrow another charity's story to move your hand. What we have is a plot being finalised, a plan the board has prayed over, and a neighbourhood where I can name, from memory, the children who will sit in the first benches.

I have watched this generation split in two for years: the madrasa took the deen, the school took the dunya, and nobody took the whole child. Nasih Public School is the mending of that split — the Book first, then a world the child can stand in, on both feet.

I am not asking for my sake. The rehal is empty; fill it, and the reward returns to you for as long as a single child recites.

The Prophet ﷺ told us to shield ourselves from the Fire even with half a date. Do not wait for a large number in your account; the first wall will be laid with small gifts, and the small ones are dearer to measure the heart. Give what you can, when you can, and ask Allah to accept it.

Come and see the plot. Sit with the board. Read the plan line by line before you give a rupee — I would not trust a man who gives without asking, and I would not keep a trustee who resents being asked. The receipt book opens on the first day, and the first audited accounts will be published whether flattering or not.

06

Where we will begin

First the neighbours we know by name; then outward, as the means allow.

The first site is Mohan Garden and the bastis along Najafgarh Road — Uttam Nagar, Dabri — where the society's founders live and where the plot for Nasih Public School is being finalised. The second circle is the Najafgarh belt: Goyla Dairy, Mitraon, Najafgarh itself. Thereafter, wherever a panchayat, an RWA or a government school writes to invite us, and the means permit.

Questions people actually ask us

Anything else — call. A person answers, not a menu.

Yes. We are registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860, and hold 12A and 80G registrations. Every donation, of any size, is entered in the receipt book and a numbered receipt is issued the same day; 80G certificates for the year are sent in April. If you donated and did not get a receipt, that is a red flag we want to see — call the office.

No. Our centres are open to every child in the village, and the registers show it — so do our benches, our boards and our board of management, three of whose seven members are not Muslim. Welfare has no religion; a child who cannot read is a child who cannot read.

There are no centres yet — and we will not show you a borrowed one. Visit the office and the plot instead: the plan, the receipt book and the board's minutes are the same on every day, and Tuesday is board day, the best day to watch the society think.

Blankets, new school material, steel bottles and sewing machines are always useful. Call before sending anything else: half the goodwill in charity godowns is stock nobody can use, and we would rather tell you what the centres need this month than store what they don't.

By rule, administration is capped at ten paise in the rupee from the first year of operations, and the audited accounts — published from year one — will show whether we kept it. Until then every rupee sits receipted and named in the build or general account, and the office stays small on principle.

The panchayat or the government school writes to us. We run a baseline reading survey; if the need is real and the village commits to providing space and bench carpentry for two years, a centre opens at the next June training. We have said no to villages, and been thanked later for it — a centre that cannot be staffed well hurts more than it helps.
