01

Why we exist

One page of memorandum, summarised in three paragraphs.

The society was started on a simple observation: in the villages around Lucknow, children were enrolled in school and yet could not read the board. So we stopped counting enrolments and started counting readers.

Ten years on, that is still the whole method. We keep programmes few, small enough to be checked by hand, and long enough to matter: a bridge class runs until a child reads, a handpump is adopted by its panchayat, a tailor earns before we call her a graduate. Where we cannot do a thing well, we do not do it at all.

We are a registered society with a seven-member board, a part-time accountant and one rule about photographs: beneficiaries are never posed with what was given to them. Dignity is a programme too.

M. Aftab Alam President, retired headmaster
Founding member, 2016
02

Six things we actually do

No tenth programme is promised. Six, done properly, is plenty.

A bridge class is ninety minutes after school hours, five days a week, in a space the village provides — a courtyard, a veranda, a panchayat bhavan room. Children are grouped by reading level, not by age; a ten-year-old and a thirteen-year-old sharing a primer is ordinary here and embarrassing nowhere.

When a child reads fluently and manages four-digit sums, the bridge ends and the school takes over. That exit is the point. Our teachers are paid a monthly honorarium, trained each June, and visited unannounced at least once a term by a cluster coordinator.

Every June, before terms begin, we hand out complete kits: canvas bag, three notebooks, slate and pencils, a geometry box, a steel bottle, and two sets of uniform stitched at our own Hunar centres — so one programme's output is another programme's input.

Distribution happens at the school gate in ordinary school hours. There is no dais, no garlanding, and no photograph of a child holding up what was given to them. The kit list and the school's enrolment register are reconciled line by line.

Six treadle machines per centre, three hours a day, nine months. A corner of every room is kept for small children, because a creche is the difference between a course and a promise. Graduates get machine time, a starter toolkit and — if they want it — preparation for the NIOS Class 8 or Class 10 open examinations.

We count a graduate as placed only when she reports income from her own stitching for three consecutive months. Last year 61% did. The rest know a trade and a creche that worked; we count that honestly too.

A one-day camp with volunteer doctors: blood pressure, blood sugar, haemoglobin, weight, and eyes. What we can fix, we fix; what needs the community health centre, we write a referral slip for and follow up through the ASHA worker's register. Spectacles prescribed at camp are delivered at the next visit, fitted, free.

We do not run a pharmacy and we do not take prescriptions from anyone who was not at the camp. Seventy-one percent of our referrals last year were followed through to a government facility; the register says so.

A broken handpump is a two-kilometre walk for somebody's daughter, so repairs come first: we stock rods, seals and chains, and train two caretakers per pump. Every repaired pump gets a soak pit so the water doesn't pool and breed mosquitoes, and the panchayat formally adopts it.

Where toilets were started and abandoned, we supply the missing door, pan or ring beam — the last fifteen percent — and the panchayat supplies the mason. 117 toilets finished this way cost a fraction of new builds and, being already half-owned by the village, actually get used.

Blankets in December and January, ration kits when the monsoon closes roads, dates and rations through Ramzan, qurbani meat on Eid al-Adha. Lists are drawn with the panchayat and the anganwadi worker, and distribution is door-to-door. A family's hardship is not a backdrop.

This is the only programme we run that we refuse to photograph. Donors who want proof of delivery get the signed list and the panchayat's seal; they do not get faces.

03

What the year looked like, counted

Every figure below appears in the audited report; the paper copy is available at the office.

Impact, in our line of work, is mostly unphotogenic: a child who reads the board, a pump that lifts water in May, a woman who pays an exam fee from her own stitching. So we count those things, and we publish the counts with their source and their date, because a number without a date is a slogan.

In FY 2024–25 the society spent ₹36.4 lakh on programmes across five districts. Bridge classes remained the largest line, as the board intends; relief remained the smallest in a normal year, and will be largest in a bad one — that is what it is for.

The society accepts no donation that arrives with a condition we cannot audit, and no foreign contribution at present. Accounts are audited by a chartered accountant in Lucknow and placed before the general body every September.

04

From the field diary

Unpolished on purpose. The diary is how the board reads the ground.

S. Qidwai · Coordinator, Hardoi cluster · 14 January 2026

The fog on the Behta Gokul road was thick enough to walk in front of the bicycle. By the time I reached centre No. 4 the children had already lit the room's two bulbs and were copying yesterday's board, which is the closest thing this job has to a standing ovation.

Rukhsar, eleven, finished her primer page and held it up without being asked. Fourteen months ago she was in Class 5 and could not read the word school when it was written in any of the three scripts on our board. Her father had decided, as fathers here often decide, that the school was a formality and the fields were a certainty. Then the school's own teacher told him, at the haat, that his daughter now read better than his son.

Nobody drops out of a class they can actually read. Boredom is a reading problem with a discipline alibi.

That sentence is not mine; it is what our founding president, a retired headmaster of forty years, repeats at every June training until we can all recite it. The evidence keeps agreeing with him. Our exit register shows the same shape year after year: attendance holds while a child is learning, and collapses the term a child stops learning — in school as in our rooms.

We are not a school and do not pretend to be. We are a bridge, and a bridge is judged by how little time anyone needs to spend on it. Rukhsar should be off our register by Holi. The school will take it from there, and the board at centre No. 4 will get a fresh row of wobbly letters, and the fog will come back next January, and we will be here.

Questions people actually ask us

Anything else — call. A person answers, not a menu.

Yes. We are registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860, and hold 12A and 80G registrations. Every donation, of any size, is entered in the receipt book and a numbered receipt is issued the same day; 80G certificates for the year are sent in April. If you donated and did not get a receipt, that is a red flag we want to see — call the office.

No. Our centres are open to every child in the village, and the registers show it — so do our benches, our boards and our board of management, two of whose seven members are not Muslim. Welfare has no religion; a child who cannot read is a child who cannot read.

Please do, and unannounced is fine — the bulbs and the registers are the same on every day. If you want a coordinator's time, give us a day's notice. Tuesdays are cluster days in every district, when teachers meet and the books are open; it is the best day to see how the society actually thinks.

Blankets, new school material, steel bottles and sewing machines are always useful. Call before sending anything else: half the goodwill in charity godowns is stock nobody can use, and we would rather tell you what the centres need this month than store what they don't.

Last year, 88.4 paise in the rupee went to programmes, 7.5 to administration and audit, 4.1 to fundraising and printing. The audited statements are published above. We keep administration small by keeping the office small: one accountant, two coordinators' desks, no air-conditioning in the storeroom.

The panchayat or the government school writes to us. We run a baseline reading survey; if the need is real and the village commits to providing space and bench carpentry for two years, a centre opens at the next June training. We have said no to villages, and been thanked later for it — a centre that cannot be staffed well hurts more than it helps.
