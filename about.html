About the society

Started with one blackboard and a retired headmaster's stubbornness.

In the winter of 2014, seven people met in a rented room above a bookbinder's shop in Mohan Garden, and registered a society with a one-line purpose: education first, and whatever a village needs to keep its children in school. This page is the longer version.

01

Twelve years, year by year

As recorded in the minute books; the books are open to any member.
  • 2014

    Md. Naim Bilal Nadvi and six others — a retired headmaster, a bank manager, a teacher, a lawyer, an anganwadi worker and a public-health researcher — register the society under the Societies Registration Act, 1860. The first act is a winter blanket distribution of 212 blankets, bought wholesale, delivered door to door.

  • 2015

    The first baseline reading survey — Mohan Garden and Uttam Nagar, 74 children counted as enrolled but unable to read the board — is carried out by Naim Bilal Nadvi, the society's founder and first full-time worker. That register becomes its founding document.

  • 2016

    The first three Roshni bridge classrooms open in Mohan Garden and Uttam Nagar with those 74 children. The first June teacher-training is held in the headmaster's own courtyard; it has run every June since without a break.

  • 2017

    First Sehat camp at the Goyla Dairy cluster with two volunteer doctors. The same year, the rule against photographing beneficiaries is written into the society's operating rules — after a well-meaning donor's photographer made a child cry.

  • 2018

    First Kitab drive: 900 kits before the term opens. The first handpump is repaired at Goyla Dairy after the panchayat asks, in writing, "can you also do water?"

  • 2019

    Roshni reaches ten centres. The June training becomes the society's fixed ritual, and the first kit list is read out at the panchayat bhavan before a single bag moves.

  • 2020

    The pandemic year. Bridge classes shrink to doorsteps and pairs of children; 1,150 ration kits go out when the lockdown closes the haat. No camp, no class, no pause in the ledger — the accounts for the year are the society's proudest paper.

  • 2021

    The first Hunar women's skill centre opens at Mohan Garden with six treadle machines and a creche corner. Sehat camps resume with the referral-and-follow-up register that still defines them.

  • 2022

    The society crosses the city: centres open in Kanjhawala and Trilokpuri, on panchayat and resettlement-colony invitations. 80G registration renewed; the first printed annual report goes to every donor by post.

  • 2023

    The first NIOS batch: eleven women pass the Class 8 open examination. Kitab uniforms begin to be stitched in-house at the Hunar centres, so one programme feeds another.

  • 2024

    Paani adds toilet-completion work with the panchayats. The 24th panchayat partnership is signed. The board is re-elected for a second three-year term, unopposed.

  • 2025

    The 9,000th school kit is handed over at Mitraon village. Programme share of spending crosses 88%. The society declines two conditional grants and records the reasons in the minutes.

  • 2026

    Nineteen centres, 2,412 children, six Hunar batches a year, and this website — which the board approved on the condition that every number on it appears in the audited report.

02

Five working principles

Read aloud at every June training since 2018.

One — Few programmes, done properly. The society runs six programmes and says no to a seventh every year. A programme we cannot staff well hurts the people it names.

Two — The exit is the goal. A bridge class succeeds when it empties; a skill course when the graduate earns; a handpump when the panchayat no longer needs us. We measure exits, not enrolments.

Three — The register is public in the village. Lists of kits, blankets and camp referrals are read out and pinned at the panchayat bhavan. Anyone in the village may check their own name and their neighbour's.

Four — Dignity is a programme. No photographs of beneficiaries, no dais, no garlanding, distribution at doorsteps. A family's hardship is not a backdrop for anybody's virtue.

Five — The ledger balances in public. Accounts are audited every year and published whether flattering or not. A donor who cannot see the books has not donated to us; they have donated to a photograph.

These principles are clauses 4.1–4.5 of the operating rules, amended 2018 and 2023. The full rules are downloadable from the home page.

04

What the society will not do

From clause 6 of the memorandum. Reviewed annually; never yet amended.

The society will not run an orphanage or a residential home; children belong in families and schools, and we strengthen both instead.

The society will not build schools; the government builds schools, and we make sure the children can read the board in them.

The society will not do political work, endorse candidates, or accept the patronage of any party; a welfare society that needs a party is a party with a welfare costume.

The society will not accept a donation with a condition it cannot audit, nor spend a restricted gift outside its restriction, however urgent the alternative seems.

The society will not photograph beneficiaries, full stop, including for donors, including for this website.

The memorandum and rules are available in Hindi on request, and in print at the office counter.

05

Who we work with

No logos exchanged, no MoUs framed on the wall. Work is the memorandum.

Every centre sits on an invitation: a gram panchayat resolution or a government school's letter. Zone offices of the Education Department receive our reading surveys; mohalla clinics and hospitals receive our referral slips; anganwadi workers co-sign the relief lists. The society's only institutional habit is to work through the people the village already trusts.
