Nasih Public School · a project of the society

Deen first. Then a dunya they can stand in.

Nasih — "the sincere well-wisher." A school revised from the ground up in Mohan Garden, because the system that schools our children was not made to keep their iman. Here the Quran is not a subject but the foundation: a child learns to Class 5, memorises the Book for two to three years, returns to Class 12, and leaves with a trade, a seat or a shop — kept reminded of Allah SWT, earning halal, serving in His way. No graduate is left alone in the dunya.

01

The idea

Adab before ilm; ilm before amal. The order is the curriculum.

The founders have watched the same child twice: at six, bright-eyed on the bench; at sixteen, bowed to the dunya — to salaries, screens and markets — and forgetting the One who provides them. The system that schools him was not designed to keep his iman; every year it penetrates a little further, into what he loves, what he wants, what he bows to. A child can pass ten examinations and lose his Lord.

So the society revised the system instead of borrowing it. Nasih Public School is that revision: a school whose whole machinery — bells, benches, registers, results — exists to keep reminding the child of Allah SWT. "I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me" (adh-Dhariyat 51:56) is the first lesson, and every other lesson is ordered beneath it. The day begins with the Book and pauses for salah five times on purpose, so that no exam ever feels more urgent than the One who taught it.

We are not against the dunya. We are against a child bowing to it. Earn halal, stand on your own feet, and serve in the way of Allah — that is the whole syllabus.

The name is the method. A nasih is a sincere well-wisher — one who advises for the other's sake, not his own. The school intends to be exactly that to every family that trusts it with a child: sincere, answerable, and present long after the result is declared.

02

The Path — four stages, one child

Each stage ends in something the next stage stands on.
  1. I

    Bunyad · Foundation to Class 5

    The full primary curriculum — language, maths, science, drawing — with Nazra Quran, adab and daily salah woven through the day, not bolted on at the edges. By Class 5 a child reads the Mushaf fluently and reads the world adequately.

    Ages 5–10 · five years
  2. II

    Hifz · The Memorisation

    The child steps out of the timetable and into the Book. Mornings belong to sabaq, sabqi and manzil under a qari; afternoons keep maths and English alive so nothing is lost. Two to three years — the pace is the child's, never the brochure's.

    Two to three years
  3. III

    Ta'lim · Class 6 to 12

    Back to the full curriculum with daily manzil revision for life: board examinations, sciences, languages, accounts. The aim is a hafiz who can also do algebra — and who sits the same Class 12 board as any public school in Delhi.

    Seven years
  4. IV

    Kasb · The Footing

    After Class 12, the society does not wave goodbye. Apprenticeships at its own Hunar centres and with partner trades, ITI and polytechnic seats, college guidance, first-job and first-shop support — until the graduate stands on their own feet, which is the only diploma the school counts. And because no one eats better food than what he eats from the work of his own hands — the Prophet ﷺ taught it, and Dawud (peace be upon him) lived it (Sahih al-Bukhari 2072) — the footing is not a favour but worship: halal money, earned standing, spent in the way of Allah.

    Until they stand alone

The hifz method, in three words

Sabaq — the new portion, heard, repeated, handed over to memory before the sun is high. Sabqi — the recent pages, recited daily so the new does not bury the near. Manzil — the old revision, a fixed portion for life, because a hifz unmaintained is a house unroofed.

04

After Class 12: the footing

The society's promise does not end at the result; it begins there.

Every graduating student sits with the Chief Executive's office before the leaving certificate dries, and chooses one of three roads: a trade, a seat, or a shop. A trade means apprenticeship — tailoring at the Hunar centres, electrical work with partner ITIs, bookkeeping with the society's own accountant. A seat means college or polytechnic, with the society standing surety for the first year's fees where the family cannot. A shop means first-shop support: the machine, the stall, the stock, and a year of the society's quiet follow-up.

The follow-up is written into the rules: every graduate is visited at three months, one year and three years. A graduate who stumbles is not a failure of the child; it is an open file at the office.

  • Electrical & repair trades, partner ITIs apprentice wage paid
  • Bookkeeping with the society's accountant two seats a year
  • College & polytechnic guidance first-year surety

05

The build, phase by phase

Money is raised before bricks, not after — the society's oldest rule.

Phase 1 is land, four classrooms, an office and a wudu area — enough for the first two Bunyad cohorts and the first hifz circle. Phase 2 adds six classrooms and the library; Phase 3 the hall and the workshop where the Kasb stage will live. Each phase begins only when its own fund closes; the society will not roof Phase 1 with Phase 2's promise.

The plot is being finalised in the Mohan Garden–Uttam Nagar belt, within walking distance of the families the society already knows by name. Title, sanction and the muttahida plan are in the advocate's file; the build account is separate, audited, and published in the annual report like every other rupee.

06

Admissions & fees

No child is turned away for money; the receipt book will prove it.

First admissions open to Mohan Garden and the bastis the society already serves, then outward as the classrooms allow. Orphans and families who cannot pay study free — quietly, from a list only the office keeps. Others pay a modest fee that the annual report publishes. Boys and girls both; from Class 9 the sections are separate, as the deen prefers and the parents expect. Uniforms and kits come from the Kitab drive, stitched by Hunar hands.

The school's character is Muslim and its door is not narrow: the society's welfare work has never asked a child its religion, and the school's neighbourly duties will not either.
